The Rise of MySpace

In the early 2000s, MySpace became the defining platform for a generation. From custom HTML profiles to Top 8 friends, it reshaped how people thought about identity online. Users could embed music, wallpapers, and glitter GIFs onto their pages — an explosion of digital self-expression unlike anything the web had seen. This is the story of how a scrappy startup from Santa Monica became the most visited website in the United States, peaking at over 100 million users and briefly overtaking Google in page views. see more...

Tom Anderson: The Accidental Billionaire

Tom was the default friend of every MySpace user, but the story behind the man and the company he built is far more interesting than most people realize. Before MySpace, Tom had hacked into ARPANET from his bedroom as a teenager — a story that would define his approach to the internet forever. After a brief brush with federal investigators, he channelled that curiosity into building products. His vision for a connected, creative web was ahead of its time, and his relaxed, user-first instincts stood in sharp contrast to the corporate overseers who would eventually take the keys. see more...

Custom Profiles: The DIY Web

Long before Squarespace and Webflow, teenagers were learning CSS and HTML to make their profiles stand out. MySpace was quietly one of the best coding bootcamps of the 2000s. Millions of kids taught themselves marquee tags, background tiling, embedded MIDI players, and z-index hacks just to get a glittery cursor trail working. An entire ecosystem of profile-editing fansites sprang up to share code snippets and layouts. It was an accidental curriculum that shaped a generation of web developers — many of whom now work at the very companies that replaced MySpace. see more...

Music Discovery Before Spotify

Before streaming, bands used MySpace pages to reach fans directly. Unsigned artists could rack up millions of plays and land record deals without a label touching them first. Arctic Monkeys distributed their first demos exclusively through MySpace before their debut album went on to break UK sales records. Lily Allen, Panic! at the Disco, and Kate Nash all built their early fanbases the same way — by posting tracks and watching the friend requests roll in. It was a genuinely democratic music discovery machine: messy, loud, and alive in a way streaming platforms, for all their algorithmic sophistication, rarely replicate. The MySpace music player was ugly and autoplay-happy, but it worked. see more...

Why MySpace Lost to Facebook

It wasn't just Zuckerberg's product instincts. MySpace's sale to News Corp in 2005 introduced layers of corporate bureaucracy that slowed every decision to a crawl. The mobile pivot came years too late, and by the time the app launched it already felt dated next to Facebook's cleaner interface. Meanwhile, a culture of spam had made the platform feel hostile to casual users. Friend requests from fake bands, phishing scams disguised as comments, and relentless ad overlays eroded the trust that made the early community so vibrant. The story is a masterclass in what happens when a platform optimises for monetisation over user experience — and a cautionary tale Silicon Valley has largely failed to learn from. see more...

The Top 8: Friendship & Hierarchy

Nothing caused more drama in 2006 than where you ranked on someone's Top 8. Demotions were read as breakups. Promotions were read as confessions. It turns out a simple ranked list was a surprisingly sharp mirror for adolescent social dynamics — surfacing jealousy, loyalty, and longing in ways no one had quite anticipated. In hindsight, it was also an early prototype for the algorithmic social sorting we now take for granted: the idea that your worth in a network could be quantified, ranked, and made publicly visible to everyone you knew. see more...

Autoplay Culture and the MIDI Wars

Every MySpace profile had a song. Whether it was Paramore at full blast, an ironic lo-fi selection, or an embarrassing emo deep-cut, music was identity — and it played the moment anyone loaded your page, whether they wanted it to or not. Opening multiple profiles in tabs was an act of sonic chaos. The autoplay era shaped how an entire generation associated songs with people, long before Spotify's collaborative playlists or Instagram Stories soundtracks made that link explicit. It also set the stage for the personal branding logic that now drives TikTok and Instagram Reels, where audio is inseparable from self-presentation. see more...

News Corp and the $580M Mistake

Rupert Murdoch's $580 million acquisition of MySpace in 2005 was hailed as a masterstroke that would finally give a major media conglomerate a foothold in social networking. Within five years it was a cautionary tale taught in business schools. The culture clash between Fox Interactive Media's top-down management style and MySpace's freewheeling, engineer-led team proved irreparable almost immediately. Product decisions that should have taken days took months. Engineers left. The interface stagnated. By the time News Corp tried to course-correct, Facebook had already lapped them — and the $580 million would eventually be written down to almost nothing. see more...

The Spam Problem

By 2007, MySpace was drowning in fake profiles, phishing links, and spam comments. The platform's open architecture — the same quality that made it so creative and expressive — made it nearly impossible to moderate at scale. Bots masquerading as attractive strangers flooded inboxes. Malicious profile CSS could redirect users to scam sites without a single click. The moderation team was overwhelmed, and the tools to fight abuse simply didn't exist yet at that scale. Users began migrating to Facebook not just for its cleaner interface, but for the simple relief of opening a social network without immediately feeling like a target. see more...

MySpace's Second Life as a Music Platform

After its social network collapse, MySpace attempted to reinvent itself as a music-focused platform under new ownership. The pivot was partial and chronically underfunded, but it preserved something genuinely valuable: an enormous archive of early-2000s indie, emo, and pop-punk recordings that artists had uploaded years before streaming existed. For a certain kind of internet archaeologist, the relaunched MySpace remains a time capsule. Tracks that never made it to Spotify, demos recorded in bedrooms, early sets from artists who went on to stadium tours — it's all still there, if you know where to look. see more...
